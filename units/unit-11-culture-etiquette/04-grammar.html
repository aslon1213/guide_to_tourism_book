Modal verbs for advice — should, ought to, must / mustn't

When do we use them? We use should and ought to to give advice — to say that something is a good idea. We use must when something is necessary or a firm rule, and mustn't when something is forbidden. All modals are followed by the base verb (ought is the exception: it needs to).

  • Visitors should learn a few greetings in the local language.
  • You ought to accept a cup of tea when your host offers it.
  • Everyone must follow the dress code at sacred sites — it is the rule.
  • You mustn't point at people with one finger; it can offend.
AffirmativeNegativeQuestion
shouldYou should bow slightly.You shouldn't arrive late.Should I remove my shoes?
ought toYou ought to bring a small gift.You ought not to refuse bread.(rare — use Should…?)
must / mustn'tGuests must cover their heads.You mustn't take photos inside.Must we book in advance?

Watch the difference: shouldn't = it is not a good idea; mustn't = it is forbidden.

Useful language — expressing opinions

Giving your opinion
  • In my opinion, tipping should always be optional.
  • From my point of view, the dress code is completely fair.
  • I'd say that hospitality is our greatest attraction.
  • If you ask me, every guide ought to study local customs.
  • I strongly believe that visitors should ask before taking photos.
Asking for an opinion
  • What do you think about bargaining at the bazaar?
  • How do you feel about the no-photo rule?
  • What's your view on tipping in hotels?

Rule of use: opinion phrases usually come at the start of the sentence and are followed by a comma; they soften your message and invite discussion.

Useful language — agreeing and disagreeing politely

Agreeing
  • I couldn't agree more.
  • You're absolutely right.
  • That's exactly how I see it.
Partly agreeing
  • I see your point, but…
  • That's true, but…
  • I agree up to a point; however,…
Disagreeing politely
  • I'm afraid I disagree.
  • I'm not so sure about that.
  • I see it a little differently.

Rule of use: in tourism, never tell a guest “You're wrong.” Soften disagreement with I'm afraid… or I see your point, but… and then give your reason.
