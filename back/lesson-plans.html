LESSON PLANS

The fourteen plans in this section give teachers a ready-made 50-minute lesson for each unit of the book, following a lead-in → presentation → controlled practice → freer practice → feedback sequence. Timings are suggestions: stretch or shorten stages to suit your group, and always set the unit Hometask writing activity so that classroom language is consolidated at home. Anticipated problems are listed with quick classroom solutions so that you can plan your monitoring before the lesson begins.

UNIT 1 Introduction to the Tourism Industry 50 min

Aims — by the end of the lesson, students can:

  • name the main sectors of the tourism industry and key job titles;
  • describe a tourism professional’s duties and daily routine using the Present Simple;
  • ask and answer simple questions about work in tourism.

Target language: Present Simple for describing jobs and routines (third-person -s, questions, negatives); sector and job vocabulary (tour operator, travel agent, concierge).

Materials: Coursebook Unit 1, board, job-title cards or slips, photos of four tourism workplaces.

Procedure:

  • Lead-in (5 min): Show photos of an airport, a hotel lobby, a restaurant and a museum, and elicit every tourism job students can connect to each picture.
  • Presentation (10 min): Tell the class about a hotel receptionist’s typical day (“She starts at 7 a.m., she greets guests…”) and use it to board the Present Simple forms, the third-person -s and question word order.
  • Controlled practice (15 min): Students complete the Unit 1 verb-form and gap-fill exercises about tourism workers’ routines, then compare answers in pairs before open-class checking.
  • Freer practice (15 min): In pairs, students draw a job card each and interview one another in role (“What do you do every morning?”), then report their partner’s routine to another pair.
  • Feedback & wrap-up (5 min): Correct the third-person -s slips collected while monitoring and elicit three new job titles learned in the lesson.

Anticipated problems and solutions:

  • Students drop the third-person -s → use a silent finger-correction signal and a short choral drill of “he checks / she books”.
  • Learners confuse travel agent and tour operator → concept-check with simple definitions (“Who sells the holiday? Who builds it?”).
  • Mixed levels in interviews → pair a stronger speaker with a weaker one and give the weaker student the question card.

Homework: Set the Unit 1 Hometask writing activity — “A Day in the Life of a Tourism Professional” (8–12 sentences about one job from the unit, using the template).

UNIT 2 Hotel Types and Accommodation 50 min

Aims — by the end of the lesson, students can:

  • describe hotel types and facilities with appropriate adjectives;
  • use there is / there are and have / has got to talk about what a hotel offers;
  • compare accommodation options with comparatives and superlatives.

Target language: Adjectives for describing places; there is / there are; have / has got; comparative and superlative forms.

Materials: Coursebook Unit 2, board, pictures of a resort, a hostel, a guesthouse and a boutique hotel.

Procedure:

  • Lead-in (5 min): Display the four accommodation pictures and have students rank them from cheapest to most expensive, justifying each choice with one adjective.
  • Presentation (10 min): Build a board profile of an imaginary hotel (“There are two pools, it has got a spa, it is bigger than the guesthouse…”) and chart the comparative and superlative formation rules underneath.
  • Controlled practice (15 min): Students complete the Unit 2 gap-fill with there is/are and have/has got, then transform sentences to compare two hotels from the unit.
  • Freer practice (15 min): Pairs invent “the best hotel in town” and present it in one minute using at least three facilities and two superlatives.
  • Feedback & wrap-up (5 min): Run delayed correction of comparative errors heard during presentations and finish with a quick-fire quiz (“Which is cheaper, a hostel or a resort?”).

Anticipated problems and solutions:

  • Double comparatives (“more cheaper”) → keep a visible board rule (one syllable = -er; long adjective = more) and point to it silently.
  • Confusion between there is and it has → contrast two example sentences about the same pool and concept-check the subject of each.

Homework: Set the Unit 2 Hometask writing activity — a description of your perfect hotel (8–12 sentences, following the plan in the unit).

UNIT 3 Making and Managing Reservations 50 min

Aims — by the end of the lesson, students can:

  • make, change and confirm a booking in a phone or email conversation;
  • choose correctly between will, going to and the Present Continuous when talking about future plans and arrangements.

Target language: Future tenses — will for spontaneous decisions, going to for intentions, Present Continuous for fixed arrangements; booking vocabulary (availability, deposit, confirmation).

Materials: Coursebook Unit 3, board, role cards with dates, room types and one change of plan.

Procedure:

  • Lead-in (5 min): Ask who has booked a hotel or ticket online and build the steps of making a reservation on the board from students’ answers.
  • Presentation (10 min): Use the unit booking dialogue to contrast “I’ll check availability” (spontaneous), “We’re going to visit Bukhara” (intention) and “We’re arriving on Friday” (arrangement) on a simple timeline.
  • Controlled practice (15 min): Students choose the correct future form in the Unit 3 exercises and reorder a jumbled reservation dialogue, checking in pairs.
  • Freer practice (15 min): Pairs role-play guest and reservations clerk using role cards that include dates, a room type and one mid-call change of plan, then swap roles.
  • Feedback & wrap-up (5 min): Replay two strong exchanges heard while monitoring and polish future-form choices with the whole class.

Anticipated problems and solutions:

  • Students use will for every future idea → ask timeline concept questions (“Did you decide now or before?”) before correcting.
  • Dates and name spelling cause breakdowns → run a one-minute alphabet and dates drill before the role-play.

Homework: Set the Unit 3 Hometask writing activity — “A trip I am going to take” (8–12 sentences about a real or imaginary trip).

UNIT 4 Check-in and Check-out Procedures 50 min

Aims — by the end of the lesson, students can:

  • manage a complete check-in and check-out conversation at a front desk;
  • make polite requests and offers with Could you…?, May I…? and Would you like…?;
  • respond appropriately to guests’ requests with polite intonation.

Target language: Polite requests and offers; front-desk vocabulary (key card, registration form, invoice, luggage storage).

Materials: Coursebook Unit 4, board, Student A / Student B role-play cards, a sample registration form.

Procedure:

  • Lead-in (5 min): Brainstorm everything a guest gives and receives at the front desk (passport, key card, receipt) onto two board columns.
  • Presentation (10 min): Work through the model check-in dialogue, underlining polite request and offer forms, and contrast the blunt “Give me your passport” with “Could I see your passport, please?”.
  • Controlled practice (15 min): Students rewrite blunt front-desk sentences as polite requests and match offers to guest situations in the Unit 4 exercises.
  • Freer practice (15 min): Pairs role-play a full check-in and then a check-out at the imaginary “Hotel Silk Road” using their A/B cards, swapping roles halfway.
  • Feedback & wrap-up (5 min): Highlight the best polite phrases overheard and drill the rising-then-falling intonation of “Would you like some help with your luggage?”.

Anticipated problems and solutions:

  • Flat intonation makes polite forms sound rude → do an exaggerated choral drill before the role-play.
  • Students mix up Could you…? and Could I…? → concept-check who performs the action in each question.

Homework: Set the Unit 4 Hometask writing activity — a blog post (8–12 sentences) about arriving and checking in at a hotel in another country.

UNIT 5 Food and Beverage Service 50 min

Aims — by the end of the lesson, students can:

  • take a restaurant order politely and accurately;
  • use countable and uncountable nouns correctly with some / any / much / many;
  • make polite suggestions about dishes and drinks.

Target language: Countable / uncountable nouns; some / any / much / many; polite suggestions (May I suggest…? Would you like to try…?).

Materials: Coursebook Unit 5, board, copies of a simple menu, order pads (slips of paper).

Procedure:

  • Lead-in (5 min): Run a two-minute team word race listing foods on a hotel breakfast buffet, then sort the winning list into countable and uncountable columns.
  • Presentation (10 min): Use a waiter–guest dialogue from the unit to present some / any / much / many with menu items and to highlight the suggestion phrases the waiter uses.
  • Controlled practice (15 min): Students complete the Unit 5 gap-fill with quantifiers and match polite suggestions to guest situations, checking answers in pairs.
  • Freer practice (15 min): In groups of three, one waiter takes a full three-course order from two guests and must make at least one polite suggestion, then roles rotate.
  • Feedback & wrap-up (5 min): Correct count/uncount slips such as “a bread” and have the class vote for the politest waiter of the day.

Anticipated problems and solutions:

  • Errors like “a rice” or “many money” → play a fast category-sorting game with word cards before the practice stage.
  • Menu item pronunciation blocks fluency → drill the menu chorally before the role-play begins.

Homework: Set the Unit 5 Hometask writing activity — the training-blog post “My Three Rules of Good Service” (8–12 sentences, following the plan).

UNIT 6 Giving Directions and Information 50 min

Aims — by the end of the lesson, students can:

  • give clear street directions using imperatives and prepositions of place;
  • ask for and give clarification when directions are unclear.

Target language: Imperatives for directions (go straight on, turn left); prepositions of place (opposite, next to, between); clarification phrases (Sorry, did you say left?).

Materials: Coursebook Unit 6, board, two versions of the same town map for the information gap.

Procedure:

  • Lead-in (5 min): Draw a simple street grid on the board and let students “steer” a chalk figure to a hidden café using only spoken directions.
  • Presentation (10 min): Trace a route on the unit map while presenting “Go straight on, turn left, it’s opposite the bank”, then add the clarification exchanges underneath.
  • Controlled practice (15 min): Students follow written directions to label places on the unit map and complete the prepositions gap-fill, checking in pairs.
  • Freer practice (15 min): Run a pair information gap: each student has the same map with different places marked and must ask for and give directions to complete it — speaking only, no pointing.
  • Feedback & wrap-up (5 min): Sort out the left/right mix-ups noted while monitoring and re-elicit the clarification phrases as a closing chant.

Anticipated problems and solutions:

  • Left/right confusion under pressure → attach a gesture to each word and drill them physically before the gap activity.
  • Students show their maps instead of speaking → have pairs sit back-to-back or place a folder between the maps.

Homework: Set the Unit 6 Hometask writing activity — a walking tour of your neighbourhood (8–12 sentences with at least six imperatives and four prepositions of place).

UNIT 7 Handling Complaints and Problems 50 min

Aims — by the end of the lesson, students can:

  • respond professionally to common guest complaints;
  • apologize with a range of set expressions and offer an appropriate solution.

Target language: Apologizing expressions (I’m terribly sorry about that; I do apologize for the inconvenience); the four-step model: listen → apologize → offer a solution → follow up.

Materials: Coursebook Unit 7, board, complaint situation cards (noisy room, wrong bill, slow service).

Procedure:

  • Lead-in (5 min): Tell a short story about a guest whose soup arrived cold, read out three possible staff replies, and have students vote on the best one and say why.
  • Presentation (10 min): Use the unit dialogue to present the four-step complaint model and board the apologizing expressions under each step.
  • Controlled practice (15 min): Students match complaints to suitable apologies and complete the Unit 7 gap-fill dialogue between an unhappy guest and the duty manager.
  • Freer practice (15 min): Pairs role-play with complaint cards, and the staff member must complete all four steps of the model before the guest may be satisfied.
  • Feedback & wrap-up (5 min): Praise the most empathetic language overheard and drill the emphatic stress in “I do apologize”.

Anticipated problems and solutions:

  • Students sound defensive or blame the guest → model “empathy first” and require an apology before any explanation.
  • The same apology is recycled every time → display a phrase menu and award a point for each different expression used.

Homework: Set the Unit 7 Hometask writing activity — an online review (8–12 sentences) about a hotel or restaurant problem, real or imagined.

UNIT 8 Telephone Skills in Tourism 50 min

Aims — by the end of the lesson, students can:

  • answer a business call professionally and transfer or hold callers politely;
  • take an accurate telephone message including names, numbers and times;
  • use repair phrases when they do not understand a caller.

Target language: Telephone phrases (I’ll put you through; Could I take a message?; Bear with me one moment); the spelling alphabet.

Materials: Coursebook Unit 8, board, blank message forms, phone-script table from the unit.

Procedure:

  • Lead-in (5 min): Read out three different call openings and have students guess what kind of tourism business is answering and what the caller probably wants.
  • Presentation (10 min): Work through the unit phone-script table, grouping phrases for answering, holding, transferring, message-taking and checking information.
  • Controlled practice (15 min): Students reorder a jumbled call, then complete a blank message form while the teacher reads a caller’s message aloud twice.
  • Freer practice (15 min): Pairs sit back-to-back and role-play two calls — booking a day tour and leaving a message for an absent manager — so they cannot rely on eye contact.
  • Feedback & wrap-up (5 min): Give feedback on clarity and name-spelling, and close with a rapid choral drill of the five most useful phrases from the lesson.

Anticipated problems and solutions:

  • Students panic without visual cues → pre-teach repair phrases (“Could you repeat that, please?”) and allow one “lifeline” per call.
  • Numbers and names are misheard → run a short alphabet-and-numbers dictation before the role-play.

Homework: Set the Unit 8 Hometask writing activity — write out a complete phone call (8–12 lines) booking a day trip to the Chimgan Mountains, using at least six phrases from the unit.

UNIT 9 Tour Guiding Basics 50 min

Aims — by the end of the lesson, students can:

  • structure a short guided-tour commentary with a clear beginning, middle and end;
  • use sequencing language (first, after that, finally) to guide listeners through an itinerary.

Target language: Sequencing language and itinerary phrases (Our first stop is…; After that we’ll move on to…; And finally…).

Materials: Coursebook Unit 9, board, a cut-up jumbled commentary, peer-feedback checklist slips.

Procedure:

  • Lead-in (5 min): Students recall the best excursion they have ever joined and tell a partner one thing the guide did especially well.
  • Presentation (10 min): Deliver a one-minute model commentary on the Registan, signposting each sequencing marker on the board as it is spoken.
  • Controlled practice (15 min): Groups order the cut-up jumbled commentary and insert the missing sequencing words, then check against the unit itinerary table.
  • Freer practice (15 min): Groups prepare and deliver a two-minute mini-tour of the school or town centre using at least four different sequencing markers.
  • Feedback & wrap-up (5 min): Listeners complete the peer checklist (audible? well sequenced? one interesting fact?) and the teacher offers two language upgrades per group.

Anticipated problems and solutions:

  • Guides read monotonously from full notes → allow only key-word cards during delivery.
  • Endless repetition of “then… then… then” → keep a marker menu on the board and tick each one as it is used.

Homework: Set the Unit 9 Hometask writing activity — the story of a memorable tour (8–12 sentences with at least four sequencing markers).

UNIT 10 Describing Attractions and Landmarks 50 min

Aims — by the end of the lesson, students can:

  • describe famous attractions and landmarks with factual, guidebook-style language;
  • use the passive voice (was built, is visited, can be seen) to describe places.

Target language: Passive voice for describing places, present and past; attraction vocabulary (monument, fortress, mausoleum, UNESCO site).

Materials: Coursebook Unit 10, board, photo quiz of five landmarks, blank fact-file cards.

Procedure:

  • Lead-in (5 min): Run a photo quiz of five famous landmarks (mixing world and Uzbek sites) — teams score a point for the name and another for the country or city.
  • Presentation (10 min): Present the passive through landmark facts (“The Kalyan Minaret was built in 1127; the Registan is visited by thousands of tourists”) and contrast each with its active equivalent on the board.
  • Controlled practice (15 min): Students transform active sentences into the passive and complete a fact-file gap-fill about a famous attraction in the Unit 10 exercises.
  • Freer practice (15 min): Pairs research-from-memory a local attraction, write five passive-voice facts on a fact-file card, and present it to another pair.
  • Feedback & wrap-up (5 min): Correct past-participle errors collected while monitoring and let the class vote for the most surprising fact of the day.

Anticipated problems and solutions:

  • Students attach by-agents to every sentence → show that the agent is usually omitted when it is unknown or unimportant.
  • Irregular past participles cause errors → run a one-minute drill of build–built, find–found, design–designed before practice.

Homework: Set the Unit 10 Hometask writing activity — a guidebook-style description of a famous attraction using at least five passive-voice sentences.

UNIT 11 Cultural Awareness and Etiquette 50 min

Aims — by the end of the lesson, students can:

  • compare cultural norms and etiquette across countries sensitively;
  • give advice to visitors using modal verbs (should, shouldn’t, must, don’t have to).

Target language: Modal verbs for advice and obligation; etiquette vocabulary (custom, taboo, hospitality, dress code).

Materials: Coursebook Unit 11, board, four etiquette statements on slips, poster paper for the tips task.

Procedure:

  • Lead-in (5 min): Read out four etiquette statements (about tipping, removing shoes, punctuality and greetings) and have students move to “agree” or “disagree” corners and defend their choice.
  • Presentation (10 min): Draw a strength scale on the board (must > should > don’t have to) and place example sentences about visiting an Uzbek home along it.
  • Controlled practice (15 min): Students choose the correct modal in the Unit 11 gap-fill about etiquette in different countries and correct a set of false advice sentences.
  • Freer practice (15 min): Groups write and present a poster, “Five Tips for Visitors to Uzbekistan”, using at least three different modal verbs.
  • Feedback & wrap-up (5 min): Clarify any must / have to / should confusion heard during presentations and pin the best tips on a class poster.

Anticipated problems and solutions:

  • Students treat should and must as identical → return to the strength scale and concept-check consequences of ignoring each.
  • Cultural generalizations become stereotypes → teach softeners such as “many people” and “it is often considered”.

Homework: Set the Unit 11 Hometask writing activity — an advice page for foreign visitors to your region, using a range of modal verbs.

UNIT 12 Promoting Tourism Services 50 min

Aims — by the end of the lesson, students can:

  • identify the persuasive devices used in tourism advertising;
  • promote a tourism service in speech and writing using persuasive language.

Target language: Persuasive language — promotional imperatives (Discover…, Escape to…), superlatives, emotive adjectives and rhetorical questions.

Materials: Coursebook Unit 12, board, two contrasting adverts for the same destination, timer for the radio-advert task.

Procedure:

  • Lead-in (5 min): Show a dull advert and a vivid advert for the same destination, have students vote for the one that makes them want to travel, and elicit why.
  • Presentation (10 min): Analyse the unit advert together, labelling each persuasive device — imperative, superlative, emotive adjective, rhetorical question — in a different colour on the board.
  • Controlled practice (15 min): Students upgrade flat sentences (“The hotel is nice”) into persuasive ones and match slogans to tourism services in the Unit 12 exercises.
  • Freer practice (15 min): Groups script and perform a sixty-second radio advert for a local tour, using at least three different persuasive devices from the lesson.
  • Feedback & wrap-up (5 min): The class votes for the most persuasive advert and must name the devices that made it work.

Anticipated problems and solutions:

  • Students borrow slogans they already know → require completely original wording and reward inventive phrases with bonus points.
  • Flat, embarrassed delivery weakens the adverts → model exaggerated “radio voice” enthusiasm before rehearsal.

Homework: Set the Unit 12 Hometask writing activity — a short promotional text for a local tourism service, using at least three persuasive devices.

UNIT 13 Business Tourism and Events 50 min

Aims — by the end of the lesson, students can:

  • describe business tourism and event services (conferences, exhibitions, incentives);
  • use formal language appropriately in business emails and conversations.

Target language: Formal language — fixed openings and closings (Dear Ms…, I look forward to…), full forms instead of contractions, indirect requests; MICE vocabulary (venue, delegate, catering).

Materials: Coursebook Unit 13, board, scrambled formal/informal phrase slips, model enquiry email.

Procedure:

  • Lead-in (5 min): Hand out scrambled phrase slips (“Hi guys!” vs “Dear Ms Karimova”) and have pairs race to sort them into formal and informal piles.
  • Presentation (10 min): Compare an informal and a formal version of the same conference enquiry email from the unit, highlighting the conventions that change between them.
  • Controlled practice (15 min): Students rewrite informal sentences in formal register and complete a formal reply email using the Unit 13 phrase bank.
  • Freer practice (15 min): Pairs role-play a venue coordinator and a corporate client negotiating dates, rooms and catering for a conference, keeping a consistently formal register.
  • Feedback & wrap-up (5 min): Review register slips heard during the negotiation (“gonna”, casual contractions) and elicit five formal phrases worth keeping in students’ notebooks.

Anticipated problems and solutions:

  • Register mixes mid-email (formal opening, informal body) → give a three-point checklist students apply before submitting.
  • Some learners over-correct into stiff, old-fashioned phrasing → provide a modern model email as the benchmark.

Homework: Set the Unit 13 Hometask writing activity — a formal email replying to a conference enquiry, following the unit email model.

UNIT 14 Emergency Situations and Safety 50 min

Aims — by the end of the lesson, students can:

  • give clear safety instructions to guests using imperatives;
  • warn about dangers using the First Conditional;
  • report an incident accurately in the Past Simple.

Target language: Imperatives for safety instructions (Do not use the lift); First Conditional for warnings (If the alarm sounds, staff will guide you outside); Past Simple for reporting incidents.

Materials: Coursebook Unit 14, board, safety signs or symbols, mock incident cards.

Procedure:

  • Lead-in (5 min): Show a set of hotel safety signs and have students guess what each means and where in a hotel they would expect to see it.
  • Presentation (10 min): Walk the class through a fire-drill scenario, boarding one example of each structure: an imperative (“Leave the building immediately”), a First Conditional warning (“If you smell smoke, call reception”) and a Past Simple report line (“The alarm rang at 6.40”).
  • Controlled practice (15 min): Students complete the Unit 14 First Conditional gap-fill warnings, sequence the jumbled safety announcement and supply the correct past forms in a short incident report.
  • Freer practice (15 min): Groups write and perform a one-minute safety briefing for hotel guests, then draw a mock incident card and report “what happened” to a partner in the Past Simple.
  • Feedback & wrap-up (5 min): Check conditional forms collected while monitoring (especially will appearing in the if-clause) and re-drill the clearest imperative commands.

Anticipated problems and solutions:

  • “If + will” errors persist → keep the formula If + Present Simple, … will visible on the board throughout.
  • Safety vocabulary (extinguisher, assembly point) is hard to pronounce → drill the items chorally before the briefing task.
  • Briefings become jokey → remind students that real guests must trust the speaker and grade for seriousness as well as accuracy.

Homework: Set the Unit 14 Hometask writing activity — a short incident report in the Past Simple describing what happened, what staff did and how the situation ended.
